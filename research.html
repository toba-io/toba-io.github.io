Research / MSc thesis

Understanding the battery
behind the voltage.

My thesis asks whether the part of a vanadium flow battery that is wearing out (membrane, electrode or electrolyte) can be found from ordinary operating data, by tracking the physical parameters of a model over time.

Skoltech · Digital Electrical Energy · Supervisors: Prof. Mikhail Pugach & Prof. Federico Ibanez

How the work fits together

One model,
three components.

Each parameter of the lab’s zero-dimensional model belongs to a part of the battery: the crossover rate to the membrane, resistance and mass transport to the electrodes, and capacity and oxidation state to the electrolyte. The two studies below look at these parts from different sides, and they share the same tools.

01 / Foundation · Year 1

A model that meets
the measurements.

In the first year of my MSc, I built a zero-dimensional flow-battery model in Simulink using the laboratory’s established physics. The model tracks electrolyte concentrations and stack voltage, can start from state of charge or measured voltage, and includes charge and discharge protection.

I fitted it to six polarisation curves and four laboratory charge–discharge cycles using a two-stage identification method adapted from the lab’s published work. A shared physical parameter set reproduced the four cycles with a mean voltage RMSE of 0.065 V in Simulink; area-specific resistance was retuned for each current. As an independent check, the fitted formal potential lands within 4 mV of the directly measured open-circuit voltage, and the fitted initial state of charge matches a Nernst estimate.

The next question was identifiability. Repeating the fit from different starting points showed that a good voltage fit does not uniquely determine every physical parameter. Formal potential, resistance, and concentration can compensate for one another.

04 / Oxidation-state study · Ten-cycle runs

The electrolyte
side of the battery.

Over time the average oxidation state of the vanadium drifts, and capacity falls with it. This study cycles the same cell with electrolytes of different starting oxidation states and checks each one by titration. It gives a measured handle on the electrolyte, the third component the model has to diagnose.

First run complete

Watching more
than the voltage.

The first run of a ten-cycle experiment investigating vanadium oxidation states is now complete. Alongside the electrical measurements, cameras recorded electrolyte volume changes and colour sensors provided an additional optical signal.

We are comparing the electrical, optical and volume records with chemical analysis of the electrolyte. Titration provides an independent reference for checking the colour-based state-of-charge measurements. The first run is complete; combined analysis remains in progress.

Wet chemistry

Preparing a reference
for the electrolyte.

To check what the electrical and colour signals tell us, we need an independent chemical measurement of the electrolyte. We first prepared the two titrants: potassium permanganate as an oxidant, and Mohr’s salt as a source of reducing Fe²⁺.

Oxalic acid provides the primary standard for checking permanganate; Mohr’s salt is checked against that standardised oxidant. The visible endpoints are loss of the purple colour in the first demonstration and a persistent faint pink in the second.

Repeat titrations and consistency checks are needed before accepting working concentrations. Chemical results will be reported with the full analysis.

Potential-monitored titration

Adding an electrical
record of the endpoint.

We add measured amounts of titrant to a stirred sample and record the electrode potential once each addition settles. Jumps in the potential-versus-volume curve help locate equivalence points for the chemical calculation.

A platinum indicator electrode follows the redox reaction; the Ag/AgCl reference electrode provides a stable comparison potential.

Our procedure specifies permanganate oxidation followed in the same beaker by Mohr’s-salt back-titration, with immediate Fe(III) fixation for air-sensitive anolyte. The aim is an independent reference for validating colour-based state-of-charge measurements.

Planned series

Vary the electrolyte.
Keep the test consistent.

The next planned series compares six starting average oxidation states (AOS), from 3.5 to 4.0. Each run uses a fresh blend of two parent stocks, with equal electrolyte mass in each tank and the same flow rate and cycling programme. The plan calls for ten cycles per condition, with the first treated as formation.

Titration before cycling establishes the actual starting composition. After discharge, the two electrolytes are pooled and titrated again to track the change in average oxidation state. Using fresh blends avoids carrying used electrolyte from one condition into the next.

This is an experimental plan, not a completed six-condition dataset. Acidity changes with oxidation state in this design, so their effects cannot be separated; cell ageing across runs also needs an order check.

05 / Main study · Protocol stage

Three membranes.
Fifty cycles each.

The main experiment compares three shortlisted membranes in one single cell: Nafion 117, Asahi (40 µm) and fumasep FAPQ-375-PP. Everything except the membrane stays the same, so differences between the records can be traced to the membrane.

For each membrane: a fresh cell build and fresh electrolyte, three break-in cycles, then polarisation curves at two flow rates, run to the limiting current. Then 50 constant-current cycles with a 10-minute open-circuit rest at the top of each cycle, and polarisation again after cycle 25 and cycle 50. Alongside the cycler, an OCV and colour reader logs an OCV cell and the electrolyte colour, and a camera follows the electrolyte volume.

Before using bench time, I test the plan in simulation (section 07 ↓).

The protocol is drafted and under review in the lab, and the cells are being assembled. There is no data from this study yet.

06 / Lab instrumentation

An OCV and
colour reader.

The lab has one battery analyzer, but two experiments need monitoring at once: the oxidation-state runs on one setup and long cycling on the other. So I built a low-cost reader that logs open-circuit voltage (OCV) and electrolyte colour on its own. An ESP32 combines an ADS1115 voltage module with two colour sensors and streams the readings to a computer. The first reader now uses soldered perfboard and a custom 3D-printed enclosure, with the two colour sensors in separate housings. Next is a second reader with two OCV channels and redesigned light-sensor housings.

The longer-term aim is to make it part of a battery management system. Each reader would then have two OCV channels, so it can measure two OCVs per cell: one on the electrolyte entering the cell and one on the electrolyte leaving it.

A prototype. Voltage checks against reference instruments are under way; validation on the battery is still ahead.

07 / Main study in simulation

Can the plan identify
each parameter?

I generate synthetic records with known parameters from the same zero-dimensional model: a 50-cycle constant-current record with the cell voltage and the voltage of an OCV cell, plus polarisation curves at several flow rates. I then fit them with multistart least squares and check which parameters come back, and how many cycles that takes.

The six parameters are capacity (Cb), crossover rate (cros_rate), resistance (ASR), formal potential (U0) and two mass-transport parameters (km_ref and beta). Under idealised conditions (the same model, simple noise, constant parameters), cycling with the OCV signal fixes the first four. The two mass-transport parameters cannot be separated by cycling alone. With polarisation curves at two flow rates added, all six came back within 10 % in every simulated case. Most of the information on constant parameters arrives within the first 5–10 cycles.

These are simulation results, not measurements. Model error, correlated noise and parameters that change over time are not tested yet.
